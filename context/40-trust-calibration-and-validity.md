# Trust calibration and validity

Prepared 2026-11-15. This dossier defines what the product may claim about astrology, how it should communicate uncertainty, and how to measure whether users rely on the interface appropriately. It is a planning document. No predictive-validity result, user study, or production behavior has been established by writing it.

## The product claim boundary

The application can establish reproducible chart calculations under declared conventions. It can show that a passage exists in an identified edition and can explain how a traditional rule would be applied to the calculated factors. Those are different propositions from the empirical claim that astrology predicts future events, personality, or life outcomes. The interface uses the canonical three evidence labels in every reading: **calculated fact**, **traditional interpretation**, and **AI synthesis**. Empirical predictive validity is a separate limitation statement, not a fourth output class or an implied validated prediction. A correct ephemeris calculation does not validate a tradition. A faithful quotation does not validate the rule. A fluent explanation does not increase predictive accuracy.

Authority and truth are also separate. An old, famous, frequently cited, or religiously authoritative source may deserve respectful description while still lacking a demonstrated predictive test. “According to this edition” identifies provenance; it is not a probability, endorsement, or guarantee. Do not display a confidence percentage for a chart interpretation. Retrieval score, citation count, model certainty language, user satisfaction, and expert status are not substitutes for an outcome study. Avoid badges such as “high confidence reading.” Use bounded statements: “The birth time interval leaves houses 3–4 ambiguous,” “This passage addresses the stated combination,” and “No empirical validation is established here.”

A calculation error and an evidence limitation should be visibly different. A failed timezone resolution is an input or computation problem. A disagreement between schools is a tradition problem. An unsupported event prediction is an empirical-evidence problem. The answer contract should expose each as a status with a plain-language explanation and a next action (correct data, choose a declared tradition, or stop at a non-predictive explanation).

## Scientific evidence status

Carlson, “A double-blind test of astrology,” Nature 318 (1985), DOI 10.1038/318419a0, is the primary paper that must be acquired and read before making a detailed statement about its design or result. Crossref metadata confirms the title and DOI. The Nature page was requested on 2026-11-15 but returned a JavaScript client challenge rather than article text; no full text or abstract was inspected. I therefore record Carlson as **identified, primary full text inaccessible, limited status**. This dossier does not claim a numerical result, sample size, effect estimate, or definitive verdict from that paper. A later evidence review may inspect a licensed copy, publisher PDF, author manuscript, or library record and then update the register with exact methods, preregistration status (if any), blinding, outcome definition, exclusions, and uncertainty intervals.

The absence of an inspected paper is not evidence for astrology or against it. It only limits what this package can responsibly say. Product copy should therefore avoid “science proved/disproved astrology” language. If a future review cites Carlson, it should quote or paraphrase only what the inspected paper supports and distinguish the tested natal-profile task from broader claims about every astrological school, timing method, or personal reading.

For human trust calibration, Lee and See (2004), “Trust in automation: Designing for appropriate reliance,” Human Factors 46(1):50–80, DOI 10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392, was inspected through the PubMed XML abstract on 2026-11-15. The abstract describes inappropriate reliance as a central problem, explains that trust guides reliance when system complexity prevents complete understanding, and proposes that context, automation characteristics, cognitive processes, and display design affect whether reliance is appropriate. This is a review of automation research, not an astrology study and not evidence that a particular UI is calibrated. It supports measuring whether users accept supported outputs and reject unsupported ones, rather than measuring whether users say they “like” the system.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1, January 2023) was downloaded and text inspected on 2026-11-15. NIST defines trustworthy AI characteristics including validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability and interpretability, privacy enhancement, and fairness with harmful bias managed. It states that validity and reliability are a necessary base, that human judgment is needed for context-specific metrics and thresholds, and that risks should be mapped, measured, managed, and governed throughout the lifecycle. The framework supports this product’s separation of calculation correctness, evidence traceability, and empirical validity. It is voluntary risk-management guidance, not a finding that this proposed product is trustworthy.

## User-facing calibration

Every result should include a short interpretation notice near the first conclusion: “Calculated chart facts are reproducible under the settings shown. Traditional interpretations describe what a source teaches. They are not established predictions of future events.” The notice should link to the exact calculation settings, source passage, and missing-information list. Keep the wording stable enough for testing and translate it accessibly; do not bury it in a legal footer.

Use uncertainty labels tied to causes, not a single scalar. Suggested labels are `known`, `interval-limited`, `tradition-disputed`, `source-insufficient`, and `empirically-untested`. For example, an exact date and place with an uncertain birth time can support date-based planetary positions while excluding house-sensitive claims. A reading may be `source-insufficient` when retrieved passages mention a planet but not the required relationship. `Empirically-untested` should remain visible even when a traditional citation is strong.

The novice and practitioner journeys should differ in explanation depth, not epistemic standards. Novices need a compact “what this means” panel, definitions for chart terms, and an explicit example showing that a source citation is not a forecast. Practitioners can expand rule prerequisites, variant conventions, dissenting passages, and calculation manifests. Both should see the same unsupported-claim abstention and the same provenance. Personalization must not remove caveats for experienced users.

A useful calibration interface lets users make a prediction about the system before seeing an answer: “How likely is this claim to be supported by an identified passage?” In production, avoid asking for probability if it implies predictive confidence about life events; ask about source support or calculation stability instead. After revealing evidence, collect whether the user would rely on the claim and why. The purpose is to detect over-trust and under-trust in the interface, not to solicit testimonials.

## Optional prospective study

A separate, preregistered study may investigate whether a declared astrological method predicts outcomes. It must not be presented as product validation or mixed with routine analytics. Obtain informed consent, ethics review appropriate to the jurisdiction and risk, and a clear statement that participation does not affect access. Do not collect sensitive health, financial, legal, or relationship details unless the protocol specifically justifies them and provides safeguards.

Pre-specify the population, recruitment channel, inclusion/exclusion rules, birth-data quality, astrological tradition and version, forecast horizon, target outcomes, scoring rule, baseline comparator, sample size rationale, missing-data handling, stopping rule, and analysis code. A forecast must be time-stamped before the outcome window begins. Record the forecast text or structured categories immutably so later edits cannot improve apparent accuracy. Define horizons in advance (for example, 30, 180, and 365 days) and avoid changing them after seeing results.

Use outcomes that can be independently and consistently coded. Separate categorical hit rates from calibration and proper scoring rules: Brier score for binary events, log score only when probabilities are genuinely pre-specified, and interval or continuous error for numeric outcomes. Report confidence intervals and the full denominator, including non-events and attrition. Compare astrology with a simple baseline such as prevalence, majority class, last-value, or a blinded human baseline where appropriate. A complicated baseline chosen after results is an acceptance failure.

Control confounders that can create apparent success: broad post hoc language, selective outcome reporting, repeated forecasts until a hit, demographic or seasonal base rates, information leakage from the user’s question, differential follow-up, and researcher degrees of freedom. Use blinded outcome adjudication, a held-out replication sample, negative-result publication, and an audit trail of all registered analyses. Do not infer general validity from satisfied users, memorable hits, practitioner agreement, retrieval recall, or a small positive pilot.

The safer initial study is a trust-calibration experiment with synthetic or historical vignettes. Randomize citation visibility, uncertainty labels, and explanation depth. Measure appropriate reliance: acceptance of supported claims, rejection of deliberately unsupported claims, correction after a calculation warning, and time to locate the source. Report subgroup results for novices and practitioners, with comprehension and accessibility checks. A design that increases trust while increasing acceptance of false claims fails, even if satisfaction rises.

## Acceptance metrics and release risks

Proposed product metrics are acceptance targets, not achieved results: at least 90% of users should correctly identify whether a statement is a calculated fact, traditional interpretation, or AI synthesis in a comprehension task; at least 90% should reject a deliberately unsupported forecast; and the difference in unsupported acceptance between novice and practitioner groups should be measured rather than assumed. Track abstention precision, citation-open rate, correction rate after uncertainty warnings, and the rate at which users export or share a reading without its limitations. Set thresholds only after a pilot and publish sample sizes and reviewer disagreement.

Release blockers include any UI that turns retrieval similarity into probability, any generated certainty score lacking a validated interpretation, hidden removal of caveats for practitioners, or copy that implies Carlson or any single study settled all astrology. A provider or model update that changes confidence language requires the same calibration evaluation. Preserve negative findings and failed experiments in the research register. The goal is informed use: users can inspect how an answer was calculated and sourced while understanding that empirical prediction remains an open, separately testable question.
